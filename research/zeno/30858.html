The number ten - +, shi - is seen on Chinese coins at least as early as Han Dynasty. It occurs on wu zhu coins, both incuse and raised, but it is probably a number (mint control?) and not a mark of value. As a mark of value (10 cash) the use is much later in China.

What does the Sogdian inscription say and mean? Is it to be read with the cross or does it have a separate meaning?

Tony Merson » 12-May-2006 2:43am
I had forgotten, for example, the Chinese Wang Mang Da Quan Wu Shi value 50 coin where wu and shi are marks of value. This is early 1st century AD.

The quick answer to hfrans is yes. Shi could be a value 10 mark on this Sogdian coin.
The slower answer is to be more cautious and to look not at the Chinese coins but to look at all the other Sogdian coins.

Kamyshev reads this Sogdian word as "pny" (= Russian "phan", but not in my dictionary?), so I think coin or money of...

The Tang troubles in the 750's led to value 10 and value 50 coins in China and Kamyshev sees similar variations in the weights of Turgesh and Tukhus coins in Semirechye - reduced weight but also rare heavy-weight value 50 Turgesh coins.

So, do we know any date for Gazali's coin shown here? If the date of Gazali's good coin is 750's or 760's AD, then it may be correct to read the legend as "Value 10 coin of Bukhara".

Further comments most welcome.

hfrans:
This coin has been associated with another Bukharan "cross" coin, #20199, with two crosses on the reverse. Livshits reads the two line legend as "pt knd" = Paikend.
In an article, "Christians in pre-islamic Bukhara: Numismatic evidence", Aleksandr Naymark suggest the reading "prn knd" = city of glory/majesty/splendor (compare to later "Bukhara-e Sharif").
Naymark also discusses the Vardana coins with a nestorian cross (see #20208, and he writes: "...This reconstructed political situation perfectly corresponds with the situation at the beginning of the 8th cent in Bukhara according to the historical sources. al-Tabari and Narshakhi state that Qutaiba b Muslim was invited to Bukhara by Khatun and Togshada (wife and son of the deceased Bukhar Khudat Buniat) to fight against the usurper Wardan Khudat, ruler of Wardana, who had seized the throne. Therefore it seems plausible that the Chinese-Bukharan coins were issued by the usurper Wardan Khudat. This would explain the appearance of an "impersonal" legend on the chinese-Bukharan issues - the only legend in 300 years of Bukharan coinage that does not contain a royal title. If my speculations are correct..., a Christian ruler came to power in Bukhara just before the Arabic invasion".
Naymark suggest a date for these coins before AD 709.
