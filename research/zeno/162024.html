Photo Details
This is a Central Asian imitation of Tang Dynasty square-holed coins with the legend ‘Kai-yuan Tong-bao’ (開元通寶).
Unearthed in Chuy (Chui) region, Kyrgyzstan.

Character 開 [kai] is written in ‘running’ script. For me it resembles the character 关 (関) [guan], but it is only my guess.

The top element of character 寶 [bao] resembles the radical 山 [shan] – ‘mountain’. Element 貝 [bei] of character 寶 features several separated dots (with lines between them). Probably, this is a pictographic character representing the local landscape – mountains, stones and rivers.

I invented a special Chinese term for this calligraphic variety: 山冠点贝宝 – “Character ‘bao’ with radical ‘shan’ on top and dots in radical ‘bei’.”

The similar coin unearthed in the same area can be seen there: Zeno # 162023.

Obviously both coins (this one and # 162023) were cast at the same mint (and probably with use of the same mold).
The question is whether this mint was of Chinese, local or even the Western Liao origin.
If to adopt the last case it could explain the possible use of the 関 [guan] character in the legend of the coin:
関元 - 'the end of (Liao) era'!

This is not made in the usual Chinese way, beyond having its pattern carved. I think it is safe to believe it is by local metal-smith. Who was not literate Chinese. The carving work is quite clear, and makes the coin a valuable artifact.

I think of beeswax because the carving strokes are too deep and broad even for pure fresh tin. It gives the coin a look like ancient one cast in a clay mold made from wax pattern, or struck from bronze die started by a process with wax plug. The question to me is, what kind of mold was used.
