Coins of this type can often be found on sales on different auctions. For example, a coin with the same inscription was sold on Stephen Album’s Rare Coins Auction (Lot 263). Below is a description of the item:
“PROTO-QARAKHANID: Malik Aram, 10th century, AE cash (4.37g), NM, A-1510P, tentative reading Malik Aram Yinal Qinqi, lovely example, Fine to Very Fine. Assigned to the region of Semirechye, now in southeastern Kazakhstan”.
An image of this coin was posted on Zeno earlier, see # 77470.

Another coin was sold on Silk Road Coin Auction. Description of the lot is as follows:
“PROTO-QARAKHANID, AE cash, c. 10th century AD. Very Rare.
Obv: arabic legend: malik aram yinal tegin
Rev: blank.
The earliest Qarakhanid coin, so identified because of the arabic script legends, which have been read as "malik aram yinal tegin" - typical turkic qarakhanid titulature. The material, manufacture (cast), and shape of the coin are of course similar to contemporary coins of the turgesh khans, and earlier sogdian pieces, all of which derived from chinese models. One of very view cash with arabic legends (aside from this type I can only think of the Rashidin coinage of 900 years later), and an extremely rare and significant silk road type. Probably the first Islamic coin produced by a Turkic dynasty”.

Dear colleagues, may I add my twopence to the discussion?
To all my knowledge, this coin type has nothing in common with Ikhshids nor with Sogd and Samarqand, as the absolute majority of such coins originates from the region of Semirechye (nowadays North Kyrgyzstan and South Qazaqstan). The words malik (above) and yinal (below) were clearly legible from the very beginning; so was perhaps aram (right) as well, although the latter one does not look 'quite Turkic' in my opinion. As for the word on the left (a title or a proper name?), first I could not make it out due to mediocre casting condition of the few initially accessible specimens: my surmise was kind of chigh(?); the others' ones, equally fictitious - qinqi, tegin &al.
Later, along with the ever-growing number of findings made recently in Kirghizia, mostly by Dr. Kamyshev, better specimens allowed to specify its reading as qarach/qaraj and confidently reject any other options and variants. Eventually, either قرج or قرچ can be treated as a normal Turkic proper name indeed. My fault is also that till now I did not update the respective printed matters and Internet postings on this topic. So please consider this comment as a preliminary publication.

One more specimen of so called ‘Proto-Qarakhanid’ coin, image of which was found on the pages of the Chinese numismatic forum bbs.chcoin.com:



What draw my attention was the description of this very nice item, below is an abridged translation of it (the full text of the article, from which this description was borrowed, can be found there):

“Four characters (words) read clockwise as ‘malik aram yinal chig’ have several different interpretations, especially the last two characters (words), regarding which there is no any uniform recognition up to now. The coin itself is believed to be an early Qarakhanid Dynasty issue. The first word, ‘malik’, means the ‘Sovereign’ (国王); the word at the right side, ‘aram’, means the ‘Great’ (伟大); the word at the bottom, ‘yinal’, stands for a name (praenomen) ‘Yinie’ (移涅), and the meaning of the word at the left side, ‘chig’, remains hitherto disputable”.

Then the author of the post briefly recounts the history of the Western Liao Dynasty and of its coinage, hereafter I provide the abridged translation of only the final portion of this post:

“… (Emperor) Yelü Dashi (耶律大石) died in 1143, and was followed by his wife, Tabuyan (塔不煙), as regent for their son ... Their son, Yelü Yilie (耶律夷列), died in 1163 and was succeeded by his sister, (Yelü) Pusuwan (普速完), who in 1164 adopted the reign title Chong-fu (崇福) ... In 1177 she killed her husband (Fuma) Xiao Duolubu (萧朶鲁不) and was executed by her father-in-law, (Xiao) Wolila (斡里刺). The second son of the Emperor Ren-zong (仁宗) (Yelü) Zhilugu (直鲁古) (also sometimes referred to as Zhulkhu – Laosa) inherited the throne in 1178 and adopted the reign title Tian-xi (天禧) (1178-1218). Coins of five different nominal values with the Chinese inscriptions ‘Tian-xi tong-bao’ and ‘Tian-xi yuan-bao’ were cast in gold, silver and copper in the first year of Tian-xi reign period. In autumn of 34th year of Tian-xi (1211) the last Emperor of the Western Liao Dynasty Zhulugu while being out hunting was captured by the Naimans headed by their leader Kuchlug (屈出律). Kuchlug usurped the Western Liao throne without changing the reign title... Two years later in 36th year of Tian-xi (1213) Zhulugu died. In 41st year of Tian-xi (1218) Kuchlug was killed by Mongols, and the Western Liao Dynasty fell.
The Western Liao coins with the Chinese inscriptions were cast strictly in accordance with the traditions of the monetary system adopted by the Khitans. The difference was that the coins with the legends written in Chinese script have been used mostly as sacrificial or donative coins or for presentation purposes and only modest amount of them were circulated as money. After the fall of the Western Liao Dynasty the majority of these Chinese type coins were salvaged by Mongol troops or taken by them as a military trophy back to Inner Mongolia area, thus the strange situation with the number of their findings in Inner Mongolia prevailing over the number of findings in the area of original Western Liao realm could be easily explained. Together with the Chinese type coins some amount of square-holed coins (and coins without holes) with the Khitan and Arabic inscriptions were cast, but they were intended for use in the spheres of ‘social life’ and trade. In 1992 a ceramic pot filled with 250 Western Liao silver and copper coins was found at the Western Liao ancient site on the territory of modern Kyrgyzstan, some of the coins were crudely made and some of them were cast perfectly. Legends of coins were written in ‘old Arabic’ script, but the period of coinage and the inscriptions themselves are unknown. It was the first finding of the Western Liao coins with Arabic inscriptions, and this fact is of great importance. In 2004 one square-holed Western Liao copper coin with Arabic inscription was unearthed in the area of Xinjiang, China. The inscription on the square-holed coin written in Kufic Arabic script used in Central Asia was already recognized. The top word on obverse is read as ‘malik’, that means the ‘Sovereign’ (国王); the word at the right side from the hole, ‘aram’, means the ‘Great’ (伟大); the word at the bottom, ‘yinal’, stands for a name (praenomen) ‘Yinie’ (移涅) or Yelü (耶律), and the word at the left side, ‘chig’, also stands for a man’s name, which, probably, is the other name of Zhilugu. The correct reading of the word ‘chig’ still remains disputable. Nevertheless, this coin was identified for sure as belonging to the Western Liao origin. Two findings of such coins confirmed the hypothesis regarding the casting of the coins with Arabic inscriptions by the Western Liao Dynasty”.

P.S. I provided this translation only as reference information and by no means I subscribe to this point of view, especially considering the fact that now we do know the correct reading of the last word, which should be read as ‘qarach’ or ‘qaraj’ (special thanks to Orientexpert) and it hardly could be associated with the name of Zhilugu (or Zhulkhu), as well as it could not be related to the name of Kuchlug (also spelled Küchlüg, Küçlüg, Güčülüg ). Further comments are welcomed.
