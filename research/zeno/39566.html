Tony Merson » 18-January-2007 12:17am
I think they are probably coins. (Especially if they were found with normal coins).

Yes, it will be much easier to cast one-sided coins in a flat open mould.

It may be on some coins the reverse is very weakly struck and not blank? In the total coins found in all types, are there many or few which look uniface? (few=errors in minting; many=all made uniface). Or are all the uniface coins cast types?

In struck coins, when there is very high relief (deep design) on the obverse, the reverse is always weak. (Even in U.K. struck pennies of George V early years you can often see the ghost image of the obverse King's head on the reverse of the coin). (This does not apply to modern medals with high relief on both sides but they need up to 20 or 30 heavy blows to dies fixed in a press).

It is possible for a coin to lose the design from one side after it has left the issuing mint. This can be deliberate defacing. But the common example is when the coins are used in games needing one side flat - "shove halfpenny" is the obvious example (the coins are placed at the edge of a marked board and then hit with the flat hand to move them - they become worn flat on the hidden side). But in these cases, the number of coins that look uniface is very small.

gazali » 18-January-2007 11:01am
I would like to add only my translation of Smirnova’s description of that type.
“Hair, almost straight cut by regular forelock, are depicted by 10 grooves. Two large spots one upon other indicate earrings made from two stones. Upon underwear (?) put on a kind of caftan with open collar. Necklace (?) on the neck.”
This issue, group III, type II by Smirnova, is cast with blank reverse. That is it’s difference from previous type I in group III, which has the same portrait on obverse and Samarqand Y-shaped tamgha in the centre and Soghdian legend around it βγy / γwβ / prn (The Lord (or God) Farn) on reverse.
It seems to me the later type II was just simplification of coin making technology. The reasons of such simplification could be quite various – from increasing amount of coins, needed in circulation up to general fall of culture level. What about myself, I’m inclinating to the former one…

gazali » 20-January-2007 12:47pm
Dear Tony Merson, here I, after all, translated few phrases about HWAB from Smirnova's book, p. 22:
"For the first time Soghdian γwβ was fixed on silver and bronze coins of Parthian circle of Bukhara oasis, 4-5th cc AD, and on drachmas, imitating coins of Sassanian Varahran V (421-439 AD), minted at first in Bukhara, later in Samarqand. In Soghdian contexts it means the head of a clan, later – the common name for sovereign nobility and as its title. The word’s etymology is unclear. One of versions, offered by compiler of this Catalogue (Smirnova here meant herself) derived it to Avestian hvapā – “somebody, whose actions are good”, i. e. blessing-doing – epithet, equally attached to deities and humans. It is unclear for us yet, should we interpret it as title or as epithet on earliest among late Soghdian coins, including Samarqand issues. On Bukhara’s drachmas it is included into double title γwβ k’w of local lords, when on preceding them Samarqand coins it means, obviously, the title of the city commune head, who approbated those coins to issue."

The title γwβ was used in Bukhara on the Asbar coins, ca AD 450/500 (but not on the Mawak coins which are slightly earlier). It was also used on the early Chach coinage (sometimes dated as early as the 3rd cent AD?), but probably minted somewhat later.
Chionites/hephtalites invaded and conquered most of Chach-Sogd about AD 350-450, maybe they introduced this title on their coinage.
